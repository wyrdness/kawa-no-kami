## Overview

Kawa-no-Kami (河の神 / 川之神, lit. 'river god'), also called Kahaku (河伯, a borrowing modeled on the Chinese river deity Hebo), is in Japanese tradition the deity (kami) of a river. In Shinto's animistic framework, every significant river — and many small streams — is understood to have a presiding spirit, and 'Kawa-no-Kami' functions both as a proper-name divinity in early texts and as a generic class shared by countless local water gods. Kawa-no-Kami is a sub-type of suijin (水神, 'water deity'). Early references appear in the Man'yōshū (8th century), where the river kami are invoked as serving the imperial table by providing fish offerings, and in mythological texts such as the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki. Folk practice attached a wide range of behaviors to Kawa-no-Kami: presiding over fish and irrigation, requiring purification rites (misogi) at riverside shrines, accepting offerings (in earlier eras, including reported human sacrifice during severe floods, later replaced — under Buddhist influence — by straw and flower dolls), and overlapping with the figure of the kappa, which is sometimes interpreted as a degraded or yōkai form of the river kami.

## Etymology

- **Origin**: Japanese: kawa (川 / 河) 'river' + no (の) genitive particle + kami (神) 'god, spirit'. Kahaku (河伯) is a Sino-Japanese borrowing from Chinese 河伯 Hé Bó, the god of the Yellow River.
- **Meaning**: 'God of the river'. Kahaku ≈ 'river earl/lord'.
- **First use**: Earliest textual occurrences in 8th-century Japanese works (Kojiki 712, Nihon Shoki 720, Man'yōshū c. 759).

## Theories & Explanations

### Paranormal / Cryptozoological

**Animist Shinto theology** _(Traditional Shinto theology)_

Each river has a kami expressing the agency of its waters; veneration sustains a reciprocal relationship between river and community.

### Skeptical

**Naturalistic / hydrological reading** _(Mainstream secular folklore studies)_

Phenomena attributed to Kawa-no-Kami (floods, droughts, fish runs) are explained by hydrology, climate, and ecology; the deity is a culturally meaningful but non-empirical figure.
